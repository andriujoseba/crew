## Round economics

Thirty-one PRs created in the window reached panel review. Across those PRs,
the API records **150 blocking verdicts submitted in the window**: **124
approvals** and **26 change requests**. Their complete timelines contain **51
round-answered signals**. These totals are derived by selecting
`APPROVED|CHANGES_REQUESTED` from `/pulls/<n>/reviews`, `review_requested` from
`/issues/<n>/timeline`, and comments beginning `📣 round answered at head` from
the same timeline for PRs #227, #228, #245, #250, #251, #254, #257, #258, #260, #262,
#263, #266, #270, #273–#278, #281, #282, #287, #288, #292, #293, #300,
#305, #307, #309, #310, and #313. The verdict window filter is explicit;
signals are counted over those PRs' complete timelines because they delimit
the rounds containing those verdicts.

Every one of the 150 verdicts has a preceding request for the same reviewer.
Pairing each verdict to that reviewer's latest preceding `review_requested`
event gives a **median request-to-verdict latency of 11m 22s**, a **90th
percentile of 23m 02s**, and a **maximum of 72m 20s**
(#228). These figures are derived by converting the paired event timestamps to epoch
seconds, sorting all 150 deltas, and taking the nearest-rank 50th and 90th
percentiles. They measure reviewer response after dispatch, not queueing before
the engine issued a request.

PR #281 is the clearest waste register. At unchanged head
`18f787e5e3ed970688761cda36a0e58e3ba8d49c`, review requests were posted at
10:35:18/19, 10:45:12, 10:55:12, and 11:05:13 after the 10:08:12 signal. The
head accumulated five change-request verdicts and one approval; repeated
change requests landed at 10:44:20, 10:50:21, and 11:00:21 after the initial
round. Two later change requests landed on head `d9d8158dd324` after the

Sources: `/issues/281/timeline` joined by head and actor to
`/pulls/281/reviews`.

For round cost, a completed round is a signal-delimited interval containing at
least one opinionated verdict; signal retries that drew no verdict are not
counted as review rounds. There were **42 completed rounds** across the 31 PRs.
This count comes from sorting each PR's `📣` comments and assigning each
eligible review after its preceding signal and before the next signal, with
only in-window verdicts eligible. Verdicts per round were **one verdict in one
round, two in two rounds, three in 22 rounds, four in 14 rounds, and six in
three rounds**, a mean of **3.45 verdicts per round**. Signal to final eligible
verdict in each round had a **median of 26m 59s**, a **mean of 32m 04s**, and a
**maximum of 1h 55m 54s** on #307. This is the end-to-end round-cost measure;
request-to-verdict latency above isolates reviewer response after dispatch.

## The board versus the truth

The named 31-PR population contains **377** `state:*` or `blocker:*` label
writes: **205 additions and 172 removals**. The dominant cycle was 47 additions
of `state:addressing`, 45 of `blocker:unrequested`, and 44 of
`state:bots-reviewing`; the derivation selects `labeled|unlabeled` events whose
label begins with either prefix in those PRs' complete timelines.

PR #281 is the measured truth gap. `state:bots-reviewing` stood from 10:20:16
to 11:26:33 (**1h 6m 17s**). The first round had already produced one approval
and two change requests by 10:35:14, but the unchanged-head signal was reused
to dispatch five further requests through 11:05:13. Thus the board said the
panel owned the move while the engine was manufacturing new panel work from a
closed round; neither `state:addressing` nor `blocker:unrequested` appeared
until 51m 19s after that first complete verdict set. Sources:
`/issues/281/timeline` and `/pulls/281/reviews`, joined on reviewer and head.

Notification and attention were different channels. The operator notifier
made **576 five-minute sweeps** over five repositories during the 48-hour
window. Only **three** items were notified, all `needs-human` PRs in ceremony;
all three were later logged as merged and their messages closed. It emitted
**zero crew notifications**, so it surfaced none of crew's four human
hand-touches below. The fleet attention path launched **15 sessions** taking
**7,280s (2h 1m 20s)**: 14 ended `ok` and one timed out. These figures come
from `dan-claude/duty/notify.log` and `SESSION END kind=attention` lines. The
yield is therefore 3/3 for the notifier's narrow merge-ready target, but 0/4
against the crew interventions that actually mattered.

## Findings and proposed 0.1.2 re-cut

The board half supports four findings for triage: spend each round signal once
(the #281 loop, already fixed by #292); suppress resume duty for a declared
park until its wake condition changes; make emergency treatment a recorded,
verifiable fleet prescription rather than two operator edits; and preserve a
claim across identity replacement without closing and reconstructing its PR.
The corpus adds the notification gap: the notifier's perfect 3/3 narrow yield
coexisted with 0/4 coverage of crew's consequential interventions. The 78.7%
zero-action rate and 26.1% busy-tick rate are baselines, not findings: a run
that launches no model session spends no measured model time, and a busy tick
shows the non-blocking lock declining duplicate work. The corpus records no
token or monetary cost per tick, so neither rate establishes loss on its own.

The largest measured unproductive-session class was **nine `TIMEOUT` or
`FAILED` sessions / 18,325s (305m 25s)**: **2.2%** of the 408 completed
sessions but **8.5%** of their 215,775s wall-clock. This in-window total is
more than four times the 3,954s parked-resume comparator, whose explicitly
different boundary runs from the park declaration through corpus collection.
The timeout total is derived from all in-window `SESSION END` lines whose
outcome is not `ok`. The 3,324s kimi review pair predates #240's fix; the
1,800s claude-reviewer attention timeout is owned by #301; two 1,800s kimi
operator timeouts precede #255; and the remaining 9,601s is two claude-triage
mention timeouts, one claude-triage hygiene timeout, and one claude-builder
build timeout. The residue is measured but not yet a specified defect; #128's
layer-one indicators already name session timeouts as the instrument that
should expose it.

The proposed re-cut on #162 should put the already-filed parked-resume fix
(#314) first, retain the spent-signal fix already landed in #292, and then
weigh the fleet-treatment and identity-transfer work against the priced
timeout class. The zero-action and busy-tick baselines do not justify a
ranking until a later run measures movement or per-tick cost. Each retained
priority is tied to measured cost rather than an unsourced judgement.
